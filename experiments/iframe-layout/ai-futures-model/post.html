The AI Futures Project, the team behind AI 2027, publishes a quantitative model of when AI might automate AI research, and how fast things move after that.{{fn:The model is live at aifuturesmodel.com, with a preset for each author's parameter guesses. This post describes the version last updated on August 16, 2026.}} It folds what used to be two separate forecasts, one for timelines and one for takeoff, into a single simulation. You choose distributions over a few dozen parameters, it runs thousands of possible futures, and it reports when each one crosses each milestone.

Underneath, the model tracks only one thing: how fast the leading AI project is making progress on AI software. The dates, the milestones and the speed of takeoff all fall out of that one trajectory crossing thresholds.

The model's headline numbers, with every parameter set to Daniel Kokotajlo's median guess, are on the right.{{fn:Setting every parameter to its median gives one representative run. That is not the same thing as the median date across the model's full distribution of simulated futures.}} The rest of this post walks through where numbers like that come from, with a few simplified versions of the model's moving parts that you can play with.

Five milestones

The model marks progress with five milestones. The first, the Automated Coder, is the one most of the timelines debate is about: the point where a frontier lab would rather keep its AI coders than its human engineers.

The later milestones are defined by research taste and expertise rather than by coding. Each is a point where a new dynamic switches on, though the same equations run the whole way through.

When people argue about timelines they usually mean the wait until AC. Takeoff is the gap from AC to full superintelligence. Depending on whose parameters you use, the model puts that anywhere from about a year to a few years.

One loop and one brake

The engine of the model is a feedback loop. AI coders add to the labor a lab can throw at research. Combined with compute, that sets how many experiments it can run. Research taste decides how much each experiment is worth. And the resulting progress makes the next generation of AI better at coding and at taste.

Pulling the other way is a brake: ideas get harder to find. The model uses the standard economist's version of this, in which every doubling of software efficiency takes more research effort than the one before.{{fn:This is the semi-endogenous growth form from economics: software progress is proportional to research effort times current efficiency raised to a power below one. The exponent is set by a returns-to-research parameter, r.}} Whether progress speeds up or stalls depends on which of the two wins, and most of the interesting parameters are really settings for that race.

Two more details matter a lot. Compute and coding labor are complements, not substitutes,{{fn:Formally a CES production function with a negative substitution parameter. The model also caps what unlimited coding labor can buy, at roughly a 15x speedup in research progress.}} so a million AI coders without more compute still hit a ceiling. And partial automation only helps so much, because whatever isn't automated yet becomes the bottleneck.

How far away is the Automated Coder?

The current version of the model anchors its timeline on coding uplift: how much faster AI makes a frontier lab's engineers. Its inputs put uplift at about 2x in August 2026, with the excess over 1x doubling roughly every five months, and an Automated Coder somewhere around 20x.

Try the calculator. The answer is only weakly sensitive to where you put the AC threshold, but strongly sensitive to the doubling time. Halving the doubling time halves the wait, while doubling the threshold adds just one more doubling.

The project checks this anchor against three others: how long the tasks AI can complete are (its time horizon),{{fn:The length of task, measured in how long it takes a skilled human, that AI can complete reliably. METR popularized this measure.}} AI company revenue (about $47B a year now, against roughly $1T for AC), and the Epoch Capabilities Index. They give broadly similar dates, which is the main reason to take any single one seriously.

The most argued-over number

The time horizon cross-check contains the most contested parameter in the model. It asks whether each doubling of the length of tasks AI can do gets easier or harder than the one before.

At exactly 1.0, every doubling costs the same and you get the plain exponential trend in METR's data. Below 1.0, each doubling is cheaper than the last, the doublings form a converging series, and even an unbounded horizon arrives on a finite date. The model's default is 0.92.

Watch the gap between 1-year and 100-year tasks as you move the slider. At 1.0 the longer horizon takes years more; at 0.92 it comes less than a year later. Below 1, it barely matters where you put the finish line, because the tail of the series is nearly free. That coupling is why this parameter draws the most criticism, and why the project demoted time horizons to a cross-check in favor of uplift.

Takeoff: does the loop outrun the brake?

After AC, research taste does most of the work. As AIs get better taste, each experiment teaches them more, which speeds up the next round of progress. The brake is still there: each doubling of software efficiency needs more effort than the last.

The model has a clean test for which one wins. If taste improves faster than ideas get harder to find, progress accelerates even without new compute, a "taste-only singularity," and takeoff could take weeks or months. If not, the loop needs compute growth to keep going, and the model expects compute growth to slow around 2028 as chip production and power run into limits.{{fn:By default, training compute grows about 0.6 orders of magnitude a year until 2028 and about 0.25 a year after that.}}

Under the model's median parameters the brake usually wins, with each doubling taking about 20% longer than the one before. But a sizable minority of the simulated futures, roughly 38%, go the other way.{{fn:From the authors' analysis of the model's behavior: the share of simulations, under median parameters, in which improving research taste alone keeps progress accelerating.}}

What the model leaves out

The authors flag several gaps themselves. Data isn't modeled as a separate input. Hardware R&D and the automation of the wider economy aren't modeled at all. And the latest version simulates the lag of training runs, since software improvements only pay off once you retrain, which made the very fastest takeoffs rarer than in AI 2027.
