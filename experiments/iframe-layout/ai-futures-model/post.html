The AI Futures Project, the team behind AI 2027, publishes a quantitative model of when AI might automate AI research, and how fast things move after that.{{fn:The model is live at aifuturesmodel.com, with a preset for each author's parameter guesses. This post describes the version last updated on August 16, 2026.}} It folds what used to be two separate forecasts, one for timelines and one for takeoff, into a single simulation. You choose distributions over a few dozen parameters, it runs thousands of possible futures, and it reports when each one crosses each milestone. In practice that means a parameter that brings the Automated Coder sooner also has to say what happens in the months after it arrives. The result is one model you can argue with, rather than two separate forecasts stitched together at the seam.

Underneath, the model tracks only one thing: how fast the leading AI project is making progress on AI software. The dates, the milestones and the speed of takeoff all fall out of that one trajectory crossing thresholds.

The model's headline numbers, with every parameter set to Daniel Kokotajlo's median guess, are on the right.{{fn:Setting every parameter to its median gives one representative run. That is not the same thing as the median date across the model's full distribution of simulated futures.}} The rest of this post walks through where numbers like that come from, with a few simplified versions of the model's moving parts that you can play with.

Five milestones

The model marks progress with five milestones. The first, the Automated Coder, is the one most of the timelines debate is about: the point where a frontier lab would rather keep its AI coders than its human engineers. The definition is a choice a lab could actually face, not a benchmark score. Benchmarks saturate and get gamed, while the question of which team you would keep if you had to choose goes straight to how much of the real work the AIs can carry on their own, including the messy parts: finding their way around a large codebase, debugging someone else's experiment, and noticing when a result is too good to be true.

The later milestones are defined by research taste and expertise rather than by coding. Each is a point where a new dynamic switches on, though the same equations run the whole way through. A Superhuman AI Researcher picks which experiments to run better than the best human researcher does. A Superintelligent AI Researcher is twice as far above that as the best human researcher is above a typical one. Top-Expert-Dominating AI does nearly all remote work at the level of the best experts, and full superintelligence is two more steps of the same size beyond that, at every cognitive task.

When people argue about timelines they usually mean the wait until AC. Takeoff is the gap from AC to full superintelligence. Depending on whose parameters you use, the model puts that anywhere from about a year to a few years. The two numbers matter for different reasons. Timelines decide how long there is to prepare before the transition starts. Takeoff decides how long there is to react once it has, and how much warning anyone gets that it is underway.

One loop and one brake

The engine of the model is a feedback loop. AI coders add to the labor a lab can throw at research. Combined with compute, that sets how many experiments it can run. Research taste decides how much each experiment is worth. And the resulting progress makes the next generation of AI better at coding and at taste.

Pulling the other way is a brake: ideas get harder to find. The model uses the standard economist's version of this, in which every doubling of software efficiency takes more research effort than the one before.{{fn:This is the semi-endogenous growth form from economics: software progress is proportional to research effort times current efficiency raised to a power below one. The exponent is set by a returns-to-research parameter, r.}} Whether progress speeds up or stalls depends on which of the two wins, and most of the interesting parameters are really settings for that race.

Two more details matter a lot. Compute and coding labor are complements, not substitutes,{{fn:Formally a CES production function with a negative substitution parameter. The model also caps what unlimited coding labor can buy, at roughly a 15x speedup in research progress.}} so a million AI coders without more compute still hit a ceiling. And partial automation only helps so much, because whatever isn't automated yet becomes the bottleneck.

How far away is the Automated Coder?

The current version of the model anchors its timeline on coding uplift: how much faster AI makes a frontier lab's engineers. Its inputs put uplift at about 2x in August 2026, with the excess over 1x doubling roughly every five months, and an Automated Coder somewhere around 20x. Uplift is a ratio: at 2x, a team using AI gets two months of work done in one. The model follows the excess over 1x, the part the AI adds, because that is the part that compounds. If the AI's share doubles on a fixed schedule, the total creeps up for a long time and then climbs very fast, which is the shape of the curve in the calculator.

Try the calculator. The answer is only weakly sensitive to where you put the AC threshold, but strongly sensitive to the doubling time. Halving the doubling time halves the wait, while doubling the threshold adds just one more doubling. That asymmetry is the main lesson of the widget. Arguing about exactly what counts as an Automated Coder moves the date much less than it seems to, while measuring how fast uplift is growing right now moves it a lot, so a better measurement of today's uplift is worth more to this forecast than a better definition of the finish line.

The project checks this anchor against three others: how long the tasks AI can complete are (its time horizon),{{fn:The length of task, measured in how long it takes a skilled human, that AI can complete reliably. METR popularized this measure.}} AI company revenue (about $47B a year now, against roughly $1T for AC), and the Epoch Capabilities Index. They give broadly similar dates, which is the main reason to take any single one seriously. If one of them pulled far away from the others, that would be the first place to look for a mistake.

The most argued-over number

The time horizon cross-check contains the most contested parameter in the model. It asks whether each doubling of the length of tasks AI can do gets easier or harder than the one before.

At exactly 1.0, every doubling costs the same and you get the plain exponential trend in METR's data. Below 1.0, each doubling is cheaper than the last, the doublings form a converging series, and even an unbounded horizon arrives on a finite date. The model's default is 0.92.

Watch the gap between 1-year and 100-year tasks as you move the slider. At 1.0 the longer horizon takes years more; at 0.92 it comes less than a year later. Below 1, it barely matters where you put the finish line, because the tail of the series is nearly free. That coupling is why this parameter draws the most criticism, and why the project demoted time horizons to a cross-check in favor of uplift.

Takeoff: does the loop outrun the brake?

After AC, research taste does most of the work. As AIs get better taste, each experiment teaches them more, which speeds up the next round of progress. The brake is still there: each doubling of software efficiency needs more effort than the last. Taste is the hardest of the model's quantities to picture. It is the skill of looking at a hundred possible experiments and knowing which three are worth the compute, and of reading a confusing result the right way. A lab with better taste gets more out of the same hardware, which is why the model treats it as a multiplier on everything else.

The model has a clean test for which one wins. If taste improves faster than ideas get harder to find, progress accelerates even without new compute, a "taste-only singularity," and takeoff could take weeks or months. If not, the loop needs compute growth to keep going, and the model expects compute growth to slow around 2028 as chip production and power run into limits.{{fn:By default, training compute grows about 0.6 orders of magnitude a year until 2028 and about 0.25 a year after that.}} In the toy version here, the two sliders decide it.

Under the model's median parameters the brake usually wins, with each doubling taking about 20% longer than the one before. But a sizable minority of the simulated futures, roughly 38%, go the other way.{{fn:From the authors' analysis of the model's behavior: the share of simulations, under median parameters, in which improving research taste alone keeps progress accelerating.}} The bars in the widget show the difference. When the brake wins, each bar is a little taller than the one before, and progress settles into a pace set by how fast new chips arrive. When the loop wins, each bar is shorter than the last, the doublings crowd together, and the whole sequence finishes in a bounded amount of time. A small move of either slider is enough to flip from one to the other, which is a fair summary of how uncertain this part of the forecast is.

What the model leaves out

The authors flag several gaps themselves. Data isn't modeled as a separate input. Hardware R&D and the automation of the wider economy aren't modeled at all. And the latest version simulates the lag of training runs, since software improvements only pay off once you retrain, which made the very fastest takeoffs rarer than in AI 2027.
